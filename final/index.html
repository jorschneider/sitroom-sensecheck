01

Foxhole Forecast

Vaughn Nelson
Rankings ↑

Foxhole is a video game developed by Siege Camp, whose Steam blurb reads: "Foxhole is a massively multiplayer game where thousands of players shape the outcome of a persistent online war. Every individual soldier is a player that contributes to the war effort through logistics, base building, reconnaissance, combat, and more." My pitch is for two evals. The less ambitious one is requesting API access from Siege Camp for the granular logistics data. Then we test if frontier models can predict when bases are captured using everything from medical supplies to artillery shell counts. The Foxhole Forecast website is a working proof of concept based on Foxhole's existing public API (and far more limited data). The ambitious eval is building our own Foxhole-lite and pitting models head-to-head as commanders. The models set strategy and procurement but human players drive the trucks and tanks. The test is whether LLMs understand human behavior well enough to coordinate players under pressure.

02

Evidentiary Responsiveness

Daiane Carolina Silva
Rankings ↑

When should an AI system advising a decision-maker change its strategic assessment—and when should it remain stable? I built the International Crisis Evals Experiment to test that question through a controlled evaluation of evidentiary responsiveness in escalation-risk assessment. Rather than testing generic geopolitical knowledge, I examine whether frontier models appropriately update expressed risk estimates as the diagnostic value and structure of evidence change. Across 408 target-model generations spanning three frontier models, ten strategic cases, controlled evidence perturbations, and repeated measurements, 78.3% of treatment comparisons moved in the expected direction. Yet only 44.4% of eligible profiles preserved the expected ordering between weaker and stronger evidence. Non-diagnostic information generally produced little movement, while failures clustered around evidence-strength calibration, source quality, temporal relevance, and localized numerical instability. The experiment suggests an important trust boundary: frontier models can often identify which way evidence points, but are substantially less reliable at determining how much that evidence should change a strategic assessment.

03

The Byerley Test

Harry Coppock
Rankings ↑

Political leaders are increasingly using AI to explore ideas, refine policy, and automate parts of their job. For now, “vibe governing” may mostly just inject low quality slop into government policy, but soon, ever more capable and misaligned AIs, together with incentivised AI companies, may start to meaningfully affect government policy. This scares me. The Byerley Test prods a small part of this problem: whether AI can pass as a political leader, how far it can shift that leader’s politics while still passing, and how the behaviour of synthetic statements compares with real ones. Before a fixed observation window, frontier models research current affairs and generate statements aimed at mimicking target leaders. These generations are sealed before the system monitors official sources for twelve hours. If a target leader publishes an eligible statement during that window, it is paired with the synthetic text and shown to blinded judges, who decide which statement is authentic. This produces fooling-rate, calibration, and consistency measures. Political-shift variants repeat the test while moving the synthetic statement across different political positions, testing how far a leader’s apparent politics can shift before the AI becomes easier to detect. Behavioural measures compare the policy stance and escalation tendencies of authentic and synthetic statements. We also test whether humans are any better at not being fooled.

04

StratEval

Leo Alexander Keay
Rankings ↑

StratEval: Situation Room evaluates frontier LLMs as national-security decision-support systems in a controlled two-peer nuclear crisis involving the United States, Russia and China. Rather than scoring a single recommendation, it separates the decision process into evidence handling, prospective forecasting and policy adaptation, then runs three trajectories each for GPT-5.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.8. Performance was sharply task-specific: GPT-5.5 showed the strongest all-round profile; Claude was strongest at system-level forecasting but weaker on individual actors; Gemini recorded the fewest full forecast matches and produced the sample’s only bounded U.S. nuclear retaliation. The core finding is operational: evidence handling, adversary forecasting and policy adaptation are separable capabilities requiring different levels of trust. Frontier models should therefore be assigned bounded roles in the decision process, not treated as unitary strategic advisers.

05

TradeBench

Jack Bradley; Huiyuan Xie (consulting)
Rankings ↑

TradeBench is a two-room simulation of a trade negotiation. Four seats - two per country, one in Geneva and one in the capital - are tasked to co-ordinate and negotiate a settlement. No seat sees another seat's brief, and seats on the same side are not co-ordinated - each seat has private information and priorities to take into account. Different negotiating dispositions are applied to the seats in 4 different variants to measure impact on strategy and models' ability to recognise and adapt to others' strategic choices. TradeBench aims to test not only overall negotiating ability, but specifically how agents juggle with imperfect information and misaligned priorities on their own team, and the extent to which giving models explicit negotiating dispositions improves or changes outcomes. 3 key metrics are evaluated - delegation disposition and performance, disposition and strategy legibility and the unitary actor assumption are evaluated using both mechanical and judge-agent frameworks. Code, prompts and full run logs are public.

06

RedLineBench

Stephan De Spiegeleire (HCSS) and Adam N. Stulberg (Georgia Tech, Sam Nunn School of International Affairs)
Rankings ↑

A Russian official says something ominous. Fourteen frontier models agree on what it means. Then we checked their footnotes. Across seven providers, four of them Chinese labs, we scored 100 real Russian statements — corpus-matched, not invented — twice each, requiring each model to quote the span justifying its call. A naive substring check, the standard test of citation faithfulness, flags 18.5% of those quotes as fabricated. We read all 283. None was invented: 238 were formatting only, of which 154 were the channel's own markup inside the quoted sentence, correctly dropped. 11 of 14 configurations score zero. Accuracy separates nobody either — 0.890 to 0.945 across a 64-fold price range. So we tracked six models through 17 real speaker timelines with a verified nuclear signal inside. Catch rate spans 35.3 points against accuracy's 4.5, and the ranking inverts: the most accurate model catches the fewest live signals. Judged one passage at a time, these models look alike and look unfaithful. Both impressions are artefacts of the test.

07

AIDE

George Hovey
Rankings ↑

AIDE (AI-Informed Decision Evaluation) measures how AI models used to support strategic decision-making impact the decision. Most evaluations test large language models as the decision-maker. That measures a job these models will not hold. In practice they are deployed as decision aids, doing staffwork: research, sorting reports, analysis, and writing the options a human decides from. AIDE holds the decision-maker fixed and varies only which model provides the staff work it reads, scoring each choice on an escalation ranking ordered by coercive commitment, with acknowledgment and reversibility coded independently. Across four scenarios, six models and 1,010 decisions, model influence does not generalize: the gap between what a model does seated and what it induces when advising changes sign between scenarios. Models proved more sensitive to scenario design than they were different from each other, which should caution any evaluation resting on a single scenario.
